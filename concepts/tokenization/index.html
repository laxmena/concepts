Tokenization

How text becomes the pieces a language model reads, and why the same sentence can cost very different amounts.

  • A model does not read letters or words. It reads tokens, pieces from a fixed list.
  • The list is learned by merging the most common neighbouring pair again and again, starting from single bytes.
  • Text that was common in the training data ends up as a few big tokens.
  • Text that was rare stays in small pieces, and every piece costs money and context.

What just happened

  1. The tokenizer starts from bytes. “” is bytes, so with no merges it is tokens.
  2. It counts every neighbouring pair in the training text. The most common here is , seen times for the first. That pair becomes one new token.
  3. It repeats. Each merge uses what the last one made, so “th” and “e” become “the”. After merges, “” is tokens.
  4. To tokenize new text, it cuts the text into bytes and replays the merges in the order they were learned. The order is the whole tokenizer.
What is simplified here

The training text is about ninety words, so the page needs tens of merges where a real tokenizer makes around a hundred thousand. Words are split on spaces and the space itself costs nothing. Real tokenizers keep the space attached to the next word and first split text with a pattern, so merges never cross a word or number boundary. Byte-pair encoding was brought to neural language models by Sennrich and others in 2015, from a compression idea.

Where you meet it

Everything a model API does is counted in tokens: the price, the context limit and the speed. When a prompt is “too long”, this is the unit that ran out. Two ways it shows up in practice:

  • The same paragraph in two languages can fill very different amounts of the context window.
  • Numbers, code and rare words split into many small pieces, which is why a model can be oddly bad at spelling or at counting letters.

Three things stack up. First, UTF-8 spends one byte on an English letter and three on a Tamil one. A written syllable like கா is two characters (க and ா), so six bytes. A tokenizer with no Tamil merges starts with three times as many pieces. Second, merges go to what is most common in the training text, and Tamil is a small share of it, so its merges come late or never. Notice that the first Tamil merges are the leading bytes every letter shares. Third, Tamil builds long words by adding endings to a root, so there are many word forms and fewer exact repeats for a merge to learn from.

Check yourself

A tokenizer is trained only on English. What happens when it meets Tamil?

It still works, because it can always fall back to single bytes. There are no merges for Tamil bytes, so each Tamil character becomes about three tokens. The text is read correctly and costs several times more.

Why not add every language and make the vocabulary huge?

Every token has a row in the model’s embedding table and in its output layer, so a bigger vocabulary means a bigger model and a slower softmax. The merges also share a fixed budget, so adding a language takes merges from the others. The usual fix is more training text for the languages that are short of it, plus a larger vocabulary, as GPT-4o’s did.

Does fewer tokens per sentence mean the model understands that language better?

No. Token count is cost and context, not skill. A model can read a language that tokenizes badly, and it will do it with less room and at a higher price. Quality depends on the model’s training data as well.
