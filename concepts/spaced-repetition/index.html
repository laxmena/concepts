Spaced repetition

A way to schedule reviews so a fact stays in memory with far fewer reviews, used in flashcard apps such as Anki.

  • You forget a fact along a curve: quickly at first, then more slowly.
  • Each review lifts recall back to 100% and makes the next fall slower.
  • A review is worth the most when it comes just before you would forget.
  • So the gaps between reviews can grow, and far fewer reviews are needed.

What just happened

What this model is, and is not

It is a toy. The 0.9 base, the starting stability of one day and the constant 6 are made up so the picture is clear. Nothing here is fitted to data. Every review is assumed to succeed, and every fact is assumed to behave the same, which the break below shows is not true.

What the toy keeps is two well-supported ideas. Memory fades with time since the last review, a result going back to Ebbinghaus in 1885. And spreading practice out beats packing it together: Cepeda and others reviewed the experiments and found that gaps between study sessions improve later recall.

Where you meet it

Flashcard apps use it to decide which card to show today. Anki asks you to choose a desired retention, the share of cards you want to remember when they come due, and its default is 90%. A higher number means more reviews per day.

  • The just-in-time rule on this page is that setting: review a card when the chance of recalling it has fallen to your target.
  • Newer schedulers such as FSRS estimate each card’s stability from how you answered before, since the right gap differs from card to card.

Where it breaks

A fixed gap suits some facts and not others. Drag the slider to make the fact slipperier or stickier and watch the blue line, a review every 3 days, against the orange one.

For a slippery fact that reaches 90% after days, a review every 3 days lets recall fall to before the second review. Just in time never lets it go below . For a sticky fact the fixed gap is the opposite problem: recall stays near and the reviews could have waited. The model also assumes every review succeeds. At recall a real review often fails, and a failed review sets the stability back.

That is why real schedulers ask you to grade each answer. The grade is the data they use to estimate that one card’s stability and set its next gap.

Check yourself

Six reviews in one hour, or six over two weeks. Which is stronger a month later, and why?

Two weeks. In the hour, recall is still near 100% at every review, so each one adds almost nothing. Spread out, each review comes when recall has fallen, and that is when it does the most.

Why not review everything every day?

Most of those reviews come while recall is high and add little, so you spend time without building much. The just-in-time schedule spends reviews where they count, which is why the gaps grow.

What does raising the target from 90% to 95% do to your workload?

It shortens every gap, because you review before recall falls as far. You remember more of your cards when they come due, and you do more reviews to get there. Anki’s own documentation says the same: lower retention means fewer reviews and more forgetting.
