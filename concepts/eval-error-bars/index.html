Is 82% better than 80%?

Error bars show how far an eval score could move if you had asked different questions. Good eval reports print them next to every score.

  • An eval score comes from a sample of questions, so another sample would give another score.
  • The range around a score shrinks with the square root of the number of questions.
  • A gap between two models counts only when its own range stays away from zero.
  • Grading both models on the same questions makes that range smaller.

Show the formulas

Each question scores 1 (right) or 0 (wrong). The score is the mean, p. The formulas below are from Miller (2024).

standard error = sqrt(p × (1 − p) / n) 95% range = p ± 1.96 × standard error gap range, unpaired = gap ± 1.96 × sqrt(SE_A² + SE_B²) gap range, paired = gap ± 1.96 × sqrt(Var(question-level difference) / n) questions needed = (z_α/2 + z_β)² × Var(difference) / gap²

With significance 5% and power 80%, z_α/2 + z_β is 1.96 + 0.84 = 2.80. This page assumes the models answer the same way every time, so the only noise is which questions were picked.

Where you meet it

A team reports that model B scores 82% and model A 78% on a 200-question eval, and ships B. The numbers look clear. The page above says the gap could be anything from to points.

  • Miller recommends printing the standard error under each eval score, as other sciences do.
  • When questions come in groups, such as several questions about one passage, he reports that clustered standard errors on two popular evals were over 3 times larger than the naive ones.
  • A worked example in the paper needs about 969 questions to see a 3-point gap 8 times out of 10, and concludes that new evals should have at least 1,000 questions.

Where it breaks

The simple range assumes the score could land on either side. A model that answers every question right has nothing above it, so the formula gives it a range with no width, as if the test were perfect. Pick a perfect score and compare it with the Wilson range, which keeps the width.

A perfect score on a test this small means little. The Wilson range for 20 of 20 starts at . The fixes are more questions, or a range that does not assume a bell curve.

Check yourself

Model A scores 70% on 100 questions. About how wide is its 95% range?

The standard error is √(0.7 × 0.3 / 100) = 0.046, and 1.96 times that is about 0.09. The range is about 61% to 79%, nine points either side.

You double the number of questions. How much narrower does the range get?

The range shrinks with the square root of n, so it gets about 29% narrower (multiply by 1/√2, which is 0.71). To halve it you need four times the questions.

Two models, both near 80%, differ by 3 points on 500 questions. Is that enough?

No. Unpaired, the standard error of the gap is √(0.32 / 500) = 0.025, so the range is about ±5 points. Paired at agreement 0.5 it is √(0.16 / 500) = 0.018, about ±3.5 points. Both ranges include 0.
